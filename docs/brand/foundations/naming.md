# Naming

## What each name is for

| Name | Use for | Not for |
|---|---|---|
| **KOOS-AYO** | The company. The full wordmark. Legal and formal contexts. | Running copy |
| **KOOS** | The product. Everything in running copy and in-product. (Brief v2, decision D6) | Legal documents |
| **The AYO Relationship Study** | The research survey, and the processing activity named in the GDPR record | The product itself |

## What it means
**KOOS-AYO means "together, with joy."**

That is the most useful fact in the brand. When a decision is genuinely balanced
— a colour, a word, a layout — the name settles it: this is a *togetherness*
product that takes conflict seriously, not a conflict tool with a friendly coat
of paint.

*Noted for confirmation:* "koos" is Estonian for together and "ayo" carries joy
in Yoruba, which lands well given the company registers in Estonia. Worth
confirming before putting that story in public copy — but if it is deliberate, it
is a good origin story and should be used.

## Why the research has its own name
Branding the study separately from the product is deliberate. Respondents are
less likely to read it as a sales form, which matters most for the
willingness-to-pay questions — the signal that is hardest to get and easiest to
contaminate. "Study" also reinforces the research framing the GDPR consent rests
on.

## The share line
**"Two People, One Journey"** is a campaign line for the marketing page and for
the text pasted when sharing the survey link.

It is deliberately **not** the survey's own title. The instrument includes
separated, divorced and single respondents, and the research plan names
"separated/divorced people willing to share learning" as a target cohort. A
couples framing at the top of the instrument would filter out exactly the people
most worth hearing from — sample bias introduced before question one.
